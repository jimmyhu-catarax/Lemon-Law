## Act I — A new car that couldn't hold a charge (2024 – early 2025)

Dr. Jimmy Hu, an ophthalmologist practicing in New Jersey, leased the IONIQ
5 new on May 23, 2024, with 40 miles on the odometer. He lives in Manhattan;
the car was registered in New Jersey, where it was kept, driven, and — soon
— repaired, always at Hyundai of Paramus.

On **February 12, 2025, at 7,026 miles**, the car went in with its first
reported failure (RO #356398): severe electrical problems, difficulty
holding a charge. The visit included Hyundai's own Recall 272 ICCU software
update. It didn't fix the problem.

## Act II — The 124 days (April – August 2025)

On **April 3, 2025** the vehicle entered Hyundai of Paramus and did not come
back until August. Hyundai's own repair order — #358530, status field
"**VEH. DISABLED**" — records the vehicle held from April 3 to August 5,
2025: **124 calendar days** (125 counting both endpoints, as the petition
does), odometer advanced three miles, while a replacement high-voltage
battery pack sat on backorder. The service notes read: *"At 100% charge
vehicle only has 41 miles of range… deviations in multiple cells."* The HV
pack was ultimately replaced under warranty.

New York's Lemon Law presumes a reasonable number of repair attempts when a
car is out of service **30 or more days**. This car was out four times that,
on the manufacturer's own paperwork, in its first year.

## Act III — The offer that should have ended it (June – November 2025)

Getting Hyundai's attention took work. A consumer-affairs case opened in
June 2025 stalled for six weeks — two referrals, a promised callback that
never came. On July 28, Dr. Hu escalated to Hyundai's executive
customer-care office; it answered within a day, and on **August 18, 2025,
Hyundai's National Consumer Affairs office offered $7,977 to repurchase the
vehicle — or $4,000 cash with the car kept — mileage deduction waived.**

Dr. Hu **accepted the $4,000 option in writing on September 25–26, 2025.**
Then came the release: a notarized waiver of California Civil Code §1542
"as well as any similar law of any state or territory of the United
States," a confidentiality clause, and an indemnity clause. He sent three
requested edits on September 30. Hyundai's representative asked for a phone
call the same day and never responded substantively in writing again; on
that call he was told the terms were "not negotiable" **[testimony — the
call is undocumented; the email requesting it is in the record]**.

By October the discussion had shifted to the repurchase — and Hyundai
conditioned closing on repair of unrelated collision damage (the October 14
"status of vehicle body repairs" inquiry is in the record). Dr. Hu
completed the rear-bumper repairs. The repurchase was never executed. The
deal that would have ended everything died over boilerplate, not money.

## Act IV — The lost vehicle (January – March 2026)

In early January 2026 the car failed again. When the dealer suggested the
cause might be a pre-existing headlamp problem, Dr. Hu paid **$2,100.30 out
of pocket** to replace the headlamp rather than argue — eliminating the
only alternative theory on the table. On **February 9** the car died in a
Passaic parking lot, fifteen minutes after parking, headlamp fully
replaced.

What followed was not repair time. Hyundai Roadside called a wrong callback
number and no tow came. The AAA driver Hyundai dispatched watched an
unauthorized tow truck take the vehicle — which Hyundai then filed as
*missing*. Dr. Hu spent February 10–11 personally phoning and visiting
impound lots until he found the car at Raineri's Towing, paid **$406.90**
to release it, and then watched three consecutive Hyundai dispatches fail
(a driver sent to the wrong address who hung up; a coordinating phone
number never relayed; an arrival 40 minutes after the lot closed). The car
reached the dealer on February 13. Hyundai later reimbursed the $391.25
impound fee — an acknowledgment in dollars that the episode should never
have happened. The repair order (#370267) recorded "VERIFIED NEEDS JUMP TO
START. TOO DEAD TO COMPLETE GDS," and a 12-volt battery was replaced under
warranty.

Meanwhile Hyundai's second offer had arrived (**January 29, 2026: $5,388.51
to repurchase, or $5,000 to keep the car**) — and quietly lapsed sixty days
later while reimbursement talks went nowhere: the daily allowances Hyundai
described on February 20 as amounts it "can consider… for possible
reimbursement" became "fixed" by February 27 and "fixed and cannot be
adjusted" by March 2. Out-of-pocket transportation costs for
January–February: **$1,793.43, receipted.**

## Act V — Arbitration, and an award that got the car's name wrong (May – June 2026)

With the second offer lapsed, Dr. Hu filed with the Attorney General's New
Car Lemon Law arbitration program in early May 2026 ($250 fee, paid May
11). On **May 11 — the same day — Hyundai withdrew the repurchase entirely
and countered at $2,500**, conveyed only through his then-attorneys, who
withdrew from the case the next day. He proceeded alone: 22 exhibits, a
personal impact statement, a Zoom hearing on June 9 before Arbitrator
Maneesha Sharma. Hyundai appeared through counsel (Danielle Alvarez
**[testimony as to the name]**), called no witnesses, and presented no
documents at the hearing.

On **June 11, 2026, the arbitrator ruled for Hyundai.** The decision:

- called the vehicle a "**new 2024 WAGON ION**";
- named the selling dealer "**Hyundai Brooklyn**" (it was Koeppel, Long
  Island City);
- cited the controlling repair order as "**RO 258530**" (it is #358530);
- attributed the February 2026 breakdown to the wrong repair order
  entirely; and
- rested on the sentence: *"Based on testimony that the vehicle is
  currently operating and no allegation that the use and value is impaired,
  the consumer has not met his burden of proof"* — when the arbitrator's
  own summary, sentences earlier, records Dr. Hu alleging the vehicle was a
  lemon, and his own arbitration form answers "Yes" to the impairment
  question.

The 124 days on Hyundai's own repair order appear nowhere in the award's
reasoning. The only explanation offered: the repair "needed back order
parts which is a challenge in whole industry," and a loaner was "provided
no charge." The statute's out-of-service clock has no parts-shortage
exception and no loaner exception.

## Act VI — Rebuilding the record (July – early August 2026)

Facing a 90-day window to challenge the award (to September 9), Dr. Hu did
something unusual: before hiring anyone, he rebuilt the entire case record
under a verification discipline — every figure, date, repair-order number,
and quotation checked against source documents, every error found and
disclosed rather than buried. The process surfaced problems in his own
papers and flagged them first: an expense ledger served in arbitration that
overstated the total by $195.82 (a Lyft daily summary double-counted); a
days-out-of-service exhibit that was listed on his index but never actually
delivered to the arbitrator; a mileage figure on his own arbitration form
that conflicted with the repair orders. All of it went to counsel,
disclosed, before anyone else could find it.

On August 3 he retained **Krukas & Vicario** — the intake attorney's first
reaction to the award: "the decision is insane" — negotiated a capped-fee
retainer, and signed on August 6. The same morning, before signing, he sent
Hyundai's executive office a documented reimbursement request for the
$1,793.43. Hyundai never answered it.

## Act VII — The petition (August 2026)

Michael Vicario drafted a Verified Petition to vacate the award under CPLR
7511(b)(1)(iii), built on the single cleanest fact in the record: the
125-day repair order that Hyundai never disputed. Dr. Hu reviewed the
drafts and caught, among other things, a wrong VIN and an overbroad
sentence in his own affirmation before signing it on August 13. **The
petition was filed and served in the Supreme Court, New York County.**

Twelve days later, Hyundai moved. On **August 26**, its internal counsel,
Jane Rigby, told the firm that **the prior $5,000 offer is back on the
table — take it or leave it** — otherwise the case goes to outside counsel
to oppose the petition.

## Where it stands (August 26, 2026)

Dr. Hu is willing to settle — on conditions, all running through counsel:

1. **The boilerplate that killed the 2025 deal comes out**: the §1542
   all-states unknown-claims waiver, the confidentiality clause, and the
   indemnity clause.
2. **The car goes back as-is** — no surprise charges for wear and tear or
   cosmetic scratches, and Hyundai closes out the lease completely
   (disposition fee, excess wear, excess mileage), with the account
   confirmed closed and nothing sent to credit bureaus.
3. The receipted **$1,793.43** and the **$250** arbitration fee, asked
   once — the incidentals sit inside Hyundai's own offer-letter language
   ("may reimburse, subject to proof… towing, and car rental costs").

The offer trajectory tells the story in four numbers: **$7,977** (August
2025) → **$5,388.51 / $5,000** (January 2026) → **$2,500** (May 2026, the
day the arbitration was filed) → **$5,000** (August 2026, twelve days after
the petition was served). The petition moved the number back up. Whatever
happens next, that much is already on the record.

*The lease reaches its contractual maximum on November 23, 2026. The story
is still being written.*
