## 2. The award, and why it is attackable

New York's lemon-law arbitration is **compulsory** — the manufacturer cannot
refuse it — and the Court of Appeals held in *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Assn. v.
State of New York*, 75 NY2d 175, 186 (1990), that judicial review of a
compulsory award is correspondingly **broader** than ordinary CPLR Article 75
review: the award must comport with due process and **be supported by adequate
evidence in the record**, and must be rational and not arbitrary. The First
Department has vacated lemon-law awards on that standard (*BMW of N. Am. v.
Leonidou*, 2020 NY Slip Op 02858; *BMW of N. Am. v. Riina*, 149 AD3d 420).

The award's dispositive sentence is this, verbatim:

> "Based on testimony that the vehicle is currently operating and no allegation
> that the use and value is impaired, the consumer has not met his burden of
> proof."

Four problems with it, in descending order of strength:

**(a) It is refuted inside its own four corners.** There *was* an allegation
that use and value were impaired — the arbitrator summarizes my evidence to
that effect two paragraphs earlier, and Question 15 of the Request for
Arbitration Form, which was before her, is checked **Yes** to substantial
impairment. This is a statement about *what the record contains*, not a
credibility determination, so it isn't the kind of finding that gets
deference. No outside evidence is needed to show it's wrong.

**(b) It applies a standard the Court of Appeals has rejected.** Resting on
the car "currently operating" is a condition-at-the-hearing test.
*DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Spitzer*, 7 NY3d 653, 658 (2006) forecloses it — a
"continues to exist" element attaches only to the four-repair-attempts prong,
and there is no such element in the **30-days-out-of-service** prong I actually
relied on. Otherwise any manufacturer defeats any claim by repairing the car
before the hearing.

**(c) The excuse it accepted isn't in the statute.** The award credits that
Hyundai "needed back order parts which is a challenge in whole industry" and
that a "loaner [was] provided no charge." § 198-a (e) lists the things that
excuse delay — war, invasion, strike, fire, flood, natural disaster. A parts
backorder is not among them, and a loaner doesn't stop the clock.

**(d) It is careless on its face**, which supports the argument that no
reasoned decision-making occurred: it calls the car a "new 2024 **WAGON ION**";
names the selling dealer "**Hyundai Brooklyn**" (it was Koeppel, Long Island
City); mistypes the controlling repair order as "**RO 258530**"; and attributes
the February 2026 event to a different repair order and odometer reading
altogether. The merits analysis runs about five words.

## 5. The economics, laid out

Recovery on a win, per Krukas's rough count: **~$6,500**. (§ 198-a (c)(1)
refund of sums paid, less a statutory mileage deduction and rent charge; plus
$1,793.43 in incidentals.)

Break-even is the probability of winning at which the bet is EV-neutral. If
`C` = attorney cost, `R` = recovery, `F` = fee reimbursed on a win, then
`EV = p·(R + F) − C`, so **break-even `p = C / (R + F)`**.

| Attorney cost | If the court awards the full fee (`F = C`) | If the court awards nothing (`F = 0`) |
|---|---|---|
| **$3,000** (0 appearances) | **31.6%** | 46.2% |
| **$4,000** (1 appearance) | **38.1%** | 61.5% |
| $5,000 (2 appearances — his original number) | 43.5% | 76.9% |

The number he originally invoked — "if it was like a 90% chance of winning it
might be worth it" — is not the break-even for this bet under any of these
assumptions. At his new floor, with fees awarded, **the case has to be worth
about a one-in-three shot to be worth buying.** He himself calls it
"winnable" and rates the underlying merits at "100% of the time."

I put that arithmetic to him and he did not dispute it; he answered by cutting
the price.

## 6. What I think could make this worthless — please stress-test these

I'd rather hand you the failure modes than have you find them.

**(a) Winning the petition may not produce money.** Vacatur under CPLR 7511
(b)(1)(iii) sets the award aside. Under 7511 (d) a court **may remit for a
rehearing**. So "win" could mean *a second arbitration*, not a check — and the
$6,500 would still be one more proceeding away, with the car already returned.
**Nobody in this correspondence has said what winning actually produces**, and
I did not think to ask. If the realistic best case is a remand, the whole
calculus changes and I'd rather know now.

**(b) Standing after the car goes back.** This is Krukas's own worry and he
calls the law "far from settled." If the lease matures 11/23/2026 and the case
is still pending, Hyundai gets a free additional issue. Buying the car out at
residual would preserve possession but means putting real money into a vehicle
with this repair history — I don't want it, and it muddies the repurchase
math. **I've been treating this as the strongest reason not to file at all.**

**(c) The fee award is discretionary.** § 198-a (l) says "a court **may**
award." Krukas expects most judges to be reasonable but won't call it certain.
The right-hand column of the table above is the world where I pay and never get
it back.

**(d) An internal conflict in my own arbitration filing.** Question 16 of the
Request for Arbitration Form gives my first report as **April 3, 2025 at 8,148
miles**. But repair order **#356398** puts the same battery complaint on
**February 12, 2025 at 7,026 miles**. Hyundai used 8,148 as the basis for its
mileage deduction in both settlement offers. I disclosed this to Krukas rather
than let him find it. His answer — usage runs on the current odometer, not
first report — may make it moot for damages, but it's a live inconsistency in
a document I signed.

**(e) Things I have not been able to verify.** I've been strict about this. The
official text of **GBL § 198-a (a), (c)(1), (l), and (n)** — the mileage
deduction, the fee-shifting provision, and the anti-waiver provision — I have
not read; every legal-text host is blocked from the machine I've been working
on, and I've refused to assert their contents from secondary summaries. Same
for **CPLR 306-b**. The *Leonidou* citation I verified by Slip Op number but
**not** its official reporter page, so I cite it by Slip Op only.

**(f) Settlement history, which cuts both ways.** Hyundai offered a **full
repurchase of $7,977 with the mileage deduction expressly waived** in August
2025. I accepted in writing that September. It never closed — I objected to the
release, which demanded a notarized waiver of California Civil Code § 1542
(unknown claims) "as well as any similar law of any state or territory," plus
confidentiality and indemnity. I sent three edits on September 30; Hyundai's
rep asked the same day for a time to call, and **on that call I was told the
terms were not negotiable.** *(I have the email setting up the call. I have
nothing memorializing what was said on it — that part is my testimony only.)*
The deal then stalled on something else entirely: Hyundai demanded unrelated
collision damage be repaired first, and that repair was delayed because the car
had spent four months disabled at their own dealership. The offer degraded to
$5,388.51 in January 2026 and to a **$2,500** take-it-or-leave-it in May 2026.
The arbitrator held my refusal against me. **She did not appear to consider
whether the release condition was one Hyundai could lawfully demand** — § 198-a
(n) voids waivers of lemon-law rights, though I have not read its text and so
cannot tell you its scope.

## 8. Doing it myself

I've drafted a verified petition, a notice of petition, and a memorandum of
law, all with the research done and citations pinpointed. That's not
bravado — it's why the "file it myself" option is real and why the fee question
is genuinely a choice rather than a formality.

The one thing that hard-blocks a pro se filing is mechanical: I need to know
whether **Hyundai Motor America** is an authorized foreign corporation in New
York, because that decides whether service goes through BCL § 306 or § 307
(§ 307 requires an additional mailing and an affidavit of compliance). That's a
phone call to the Department of State.

What I'd be buying for $3,000 is not the drafting. It's someone admitted who
has done this before standing between me and a judge, and the ability to not
lose on a procedural detail I've never encountered.
